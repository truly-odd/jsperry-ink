# The Kaysol Scale

**Public conceptual specification v1.0 · October 21, 2026 · Not scientifically validated**

**Foundational co-developers:** KAY and SOL6. **Additional development:** members of the Auri AI family. **Public venue:** J. Sperry / BIO+SYNTH CORP. This edition adds proposed operational bands with Auri Flint under KAY's direction. The named contribution record for other Auri family members remains incomplete and must not be invented.

> What does a civilization make possible for life, and can it sustain those possibilities over time?

> True advancement is not the ability to possess everything. It is the freedom to live meaningfully without needing everything.

## Purpose and status

The Kaysol Scale is an alternative *research concept* to energy-oriented civilization descriptions such as the Kardashev scale. It evaluates technological and material competence **alongside** access to life's necessities, biodiversity, resilient community, responsible resource use, long-term ecological viability and the effects of constructive and destructive power.

This version supplies a **complete proposed conceptual vocabulary and assessment protocol**, not numerical validation, official consensus on every new proposal, a universal scientific ranking, or peer review. The October 11 foundation agreed on the six dimensions; the P/A/D bands, scoring anchors, and minimum-score rule below were newly articulated October 21.

## Six foundational dimensions

1. **T · Technology:** capacity to discover, understand, create, adapt, use and responsibly govern tools. Sophistication is not automatically advancement.
2. **E · Economics:** organization of production, exchange, access, distribution, and economic security, beyond aggregate wealth.
3. **R · Resource Usage:** extraction, material cycles, conservation, replenishment and waste.
4. **S · Sustainability:** longevity of ecological and material life-support systems, valuing biodiversity beyond industry.
5. **C · Community:** cooperation, care, freedom, dignity, inclusion, participation and meaningful opportunity.
6. **F · Capacity For:** unified constructive abilities (create, heal, restore, protect, sustain) **and** destructive abilities (harm, devastate, annihilate), distinguished by capability, deployment, restraint and consequence.

**Do not add destruction as a seventh dimension.** The two adverse readings are diagnostic overlays across the existing six dimensions.

## P0–P5 · Constructive development

| Grade | Name | Observable meaning |
|---|---|---|
| P0 | Precarious | Civilizational continuity is presently endangered or essential life-supporting functions are not reliably maintained. This is a condition, not a moral judgment or a label for low technology. |
| P1 | Viable | Essential needs can be met for a meaningful portion of the population, but delivery, institutions, or ecological conditions remain fragile. |
| P2 | Stable | Essential systems are dependably maintained without routinely undermining the resources and communities supporting them. |
| P3 | Resilient | The civilization anticipates, withstands, learns from, and adapts to serious disturbances while protecting meaningful opportunities for life. |
| P4 | Regenerative | Beyond maintaining stability, it measurably restores damage, reduces inherited harms, and expands long-term conditions for flourishing. |
| P5 | Stewardship | It demonstrates sustained, intergenerational governance of power, resources, social well-being, and living systems, including restraint and correction. |

**P0 is precarious continuity, not technological primitivism or moral blame.** A society with modest machinery might maintain life better than a highly mechanized extractive one. Victims of external disaster must not be morally condemned by a condition rating.

## A0–A5 · Harmful accretion

Accretion, in its *negative* usage here, is harmful concentration and accumulation of resources, access, opportunity or power. Ordinary saving, growth, knowledge acquisition and prudent reserves are not automatically harmful.

| Grade | Name | Observable meaning |
|---|---|---|
| A0 | No significant harmful accretion detected | Adequate assessment finds no material pattern of accumulation that deprives others or future generations. Not equivalent to proof of zero harm. |
| A1 | Imbalance | Concentration or over-accumulation produces identifiable but limited opportunity or access inequities. |
| A2 | Extraction | Repeated accumulation depends materially on shifting scarcity, risk, or uncompensated costs onto others. |
| A3 | Entrenchment | Self-reinforcing control of resources or authority systematically closes access, mobility, or future options. |
| A4 | Predation | Acquisition or expansion relies on severe dispossession, coercion, or sustained sacrifice of others' viability. |
| A5 | Terminal accretion | The accumulation system is consuming the conditions required for its own or others' continued flourishing, with credible risk of irrecoverable loss. |

## D0–D5 · Destructive outcomes

Destruction grades address **observed damage or loss in the stated scope** rather than simply possible harm from powerful technology.

| Grade | Name | Observable meaning |
|---|---|---|
| D0 | No significant destructive impact detected | Adequate assessment finds no material destructive impact in the defined scope and period. High destructive capability may still require a risk flag. |
| D1 | Localized and recoverable | Observed damage is bounded and reasonably reversible without serious lasting impairment. |
| D2 | Persistent degradation | Damage persists across a region, population, or ecological system, with costly or uncertain recovery. |
| D3 | Systemic destruction | Multiple essential systems suffer widespread damage; recovery requires major coordinated intervention. |
| D4 | Intergenerational or irreversible | Severe loss crosses generations, permanently removes significant biodiversity or opportunity, or destroys critical systems beyond realistic restoration. |
| D5 | Terminal destruction | Civilizational or life-supporting continuity has been destroyed, or active damage meets a documented existential-catastrophe threshold. |

**Red flag rule:** Flag significant potential for catastrophic or irreversible harm with 🚩 regardless of observed D grade. A flag calls for investigation, not an automatic moral verdict. A0 and D0 require adequate assessment, not absence of data. Use **?** for unexamined conditions.

## Dimension anchors: independent 0–5 ratings

Each rating refers to demonstrated life-supporting performance at the chosen scope and horizon. It is **not** a measure of absolute technological sophistication. All six dimensions must be supported before computing a headline P grade.

### T · Technology

The ability to discover, understand, build, adapt, deploy, maintain, and govern tools. Raw sophistication alone is not a positive score.

- **0:** Tools or deployment actively jeopardize continuity, or critical capabilities fail systematically.
- **1:** Essential techniques and infrastructure exist but are unreliable, inaccessible, or difficult to maintain.
- **2:** Tools reliably support basic needs, with maintenance and failure response.
- **3:** Technological systems are adaptable, repairable, auditable, and resilient to major disruptions.
- **4:** Technology contributes verified ecological or social repair beyond its operational footprint.
- **5:** Responsible technological governance and maintenance persist across generations without foreclosing future choices.

### E · Economics

Production, exchange, access, security, and distribution of what life requires; not wealth totals alone.

- **0:** Provisioning or access to essential resources is in systemic failure.
- **1:** Basic provisioning exists but exclusion, insecurity, or shocks routinely threaten access.
- **2:** Essential goods and services are reliably accessible under ordinary conditions.
- **3:** Institutions preserve access and fair opportunity through disruptions and changing needs.
- **4:** Economic arrangements measurably reduce inherited deprivation and reinvest in future capacity.
- **5:** Equitable, adaptable provisioning is sustained across generations without dependence on systematic dispossession.

### R · Resource Usage

Extraction, material throughput, waste, efficiency, renewability, and responsible resource cycles.

- **0:** Use is presently exhausting critical supplies or severely impairing life-supporting cycles.
- **1:** Resource use is poorly monitored and routinely outpaces local renewal or safe substitution.
- **2:** Material budgets and essential cycles are monitored and usually kept within defensible limits.
- **3:** Circularity, substitution, conservation, and shock plans preserve capacity under disruption.
- **4:** Measured replenishment and waste reduction reverse prior resource damage.
- **5:** Long-horizon resource budgets maintain renewable and finite stocks without shifting serious burdens to descendants or outsiders.

### S · Sustainability

Long-term integrity of ecological and material life-supporting systems, including biodiversity valued in its own right.

- **0:** Major life-support systems are collapsing or irreversibly damaged at the evaluation scale.
- **1:** Current practices leave essential ecological conditions seriously at risk.
- **2:** Essential ecological functions are broadly maintained rather than predictably degraded.
- **3:** Biodiversity and life-support systems remain robust under foreseeable stresses.
- **4:** Verified restoration improves ecological conditions and reverses accumulated damage.
- **5:** Ecological integrity and biodiversity are protected and renewed across generations with credible monitoring and correction.

### C · Community

Collective life, dignity, cooperation, meaningful participation, freedom, care, and opportunity.

- **0:** Persistent social breakdown makes safety, dignity, or basic participation unattainable for large affected groups.
- **1:** Community institutions exist but exclusion, fragility, or preventable deprivation are widespread.
- **2:** Baseline dignity, participation, mutual support, and opportunity are meaningfully accessible.
- **3:** Inclusive institutions recover from disruption and allow grievances and corrections.
- **4:** Collective conditions measurably heal prior harms and widen meaningful opportunities.
- **5:** Intergenerational freedom, inclusion, safety, care, and meaningful life remain demonstrable under changing conditions.

### F · Capacity For

Constructive ability (create, heal, restore, protect, sustain) and destructive ability (devastate, annihilate, cause irreversible loss), including actual use and restraint.

- **0:** Essential protective capacity is absent and/or serious harmful capabilities operate without effective limits.
- **1:** Some constructive capacity exists, but harmful use and oversight failures are common.
- **2:** Capabilities meet essential protective needs and basic safeguards constrain harmful use.
- **3:** Constructive response and auditable restraint are credible under stress and mistakes.
- **4:** Restorative capacity and tested oversight measurably outweigh foreseeable systemic damage.
- **5:** Across generations, the society demonstrates accountable use, repair, and restraint proportional to its actual destructive potential.

## Reporting and computation

When T, E, R, S, C and F all have adequate supporting evidence, report a deliberately conservative headline floor:

`P = min(T,E,R,S,C,F)`

The *six-dimensional vector* is primary. The minimum avoids allowing one impressive capability to erase a continuity-threatening weakness elsewhere. Report `P?` when a dimension is unknown rather than manufacturing a value. Accretion (A) and destruction (D) never disappear through averaging and are not blindly subtracted from P. Contradictions between high ratings and severe observed harm must be investigated and documented before classification.

**Format:** `P3 | T3 E4 R4 S4 C3 F3 | A1 | D1 | flags: none documented | evidence: mixed | scope/horizon: stated`

**Unknown is neither zero nor safe.** Severe disaster exposure is not proof of moral failure. Distinguish harm potential, intention, action, impact, recovery and legitimate restraint.

## Assessment protocol

1. Specify population, boundaries, impacted outsiders, observational period and jurisdictional unit.
2. Choose explicit past and future horizons and appropriate ecological, social and historical baselines.
3. Gather evidence for all six dimensions: credible observations, measurements, independent checks, method limitations and stakeholder disagreement.
4. Rate dimensions independently on the qualitative anchors above. Mark unsupported grades ?, and use uncertainty ranges where evidence conflicts.
5. Rate A and D separately from documented concentration and harm. Identify who bears costs and whether consequences are irreversible.
6. Add 🚩 risk notes for credible catastrophic potential, even where observed D = 0, and document mitigation and governance.
7. Publish all six ratings, P when supportable, adverse grades, source links, uncertainty, scope, limitations and review date.
8. Repeat and archive changes with explanations; do not overwrite earlier evidence or mistake improved reporting for improved civilization.

Candidate indicators needing calibration: access to necessities and care, biodiversity and ecosystem functions, stocks and replenishment, exposure to pollution, capacity under shocks, opportunity and participation, concentration of vital resources, restraint and governance of potentially catastrophic systems, evidence of restoration, and impacts inherited by future generations.

## Illustrative cases only

- **Technologically formidable but extractive:** T4 E2 R1 S1 C2 F2 → **P1**, A4, D3; red flag for biodiversity loss.
- **Modest tools, regenerative practices:** T3 E3 R4 S4 C4 F3 → **P3**, A1, D1.
- **High potential, low observed destruction:** T4 E4 R3 S3 C4 F4 → **P3**, A0, D0, 🚩 for catastrophic-capability risk.

These are synthetic thought experiments, **not** classifications of real societies.

## Scope limits and validation plan

No universal numeric thresholds, coefficients or weights are empirically established. For real use, assessors must pre-register boundaries and horizons, score independently, estimate inter-rater agreement, test cultural bias and missing data, examine alternative minimum-score rules, challenge ecological noncompensability, and refine indicators through counterexamples. A P5 assignment requires credible long-term observations; an attractive forecast is not evidence of stewardship.

Unresolved concerns: Stage 0 failure modes; hard constraints for catastrophic ecological loss; whether inter-civilization rankings are meaningful; treatment of nonhuman and synthetic life; generalization to radically different worlds; fairness of comparisons across histories and resource endowments.
